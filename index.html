I am an Associate Professor of Latinx Studies in the Department of Ethnic Studies at the University of California, Berkeley, where I serve as coordinator of the Chicanx Latinx Studies Program. I am also a faculty fellow with the Latino Social Science Pipeline Initiative and participate in the Latinxs and Democracy Cluster.

My research examines the racial classification, identification, and stratification of Latinxs in the United States, with particular attention to higher education, immigration politics, and the relationship between racial data and racial equity.

As a research program, my questions began at the individual level. Many Americans identify with one racial category while being seen and treated as members of another, an experience I refer to as racialized misalignment. The misalignment I study is structured, patterned, and deeply consequential.

Over time, my work has traced parallel dynamics upward into higher education. Colleges and universities claim identities for themselves, receive racialized classifications from the state, elicit identification from students, faculty, and staff, and then experience these contested identities in racialized ways. Organizations, like people, experience racialization.

I have served on the U.S. Census Bureau’s National Advisory Committee on Racial, Ethnic, and Other Populations, as Chair of the American Sociological Association’s Section on Racial and Ethnic Inequality, and as a member of the Executive Committee of the American Sociological Association.

Research

My scholarship sits at the intersection of sociology and Latinx Studies and is organized around four connected themes.

Latinx Racialization: Classification, Contestation, and Ideology

How are Latinxs racialized in the United States, and what happens when different dimensions of race don’t line up? This work examines misalignments among self-identification, how others categorize Latinxs, and racialized experiences, all of which are deeply patterned by structural inequalities.

Hispanic-Serving Institutions and Latinx Studies as Institutional Infrastructure

The same general structure of racialization extends, too, to organizations. Colleges claim racialized identities in their missions, receive classifications from the state through designations like Hispanic-Serving Institution, elicit identification from students and staff, and then experience these contested identities in racialized ways. My work on HSIs explores how racialization operates at the institutional level, and how Latinx Studies can serve as infrastructure for supporting Latinx students and communities.

Immigration Politics, Enforcement, and Public Opinion

Most recently my work has turned towards immigration. I am interested in questions like: what shapes attitudes toward immigrants, and what do enforcement regimes do to the communities they target? What are the consequences of immigration enforcement on the well-being, mobility, and health of immigrant families and their neighbors?

Race, Data, and Measurement

Cutting across all of this work is a methodological commitment to think critically about what race data can and cannot do. My empirical work consistently asks how categories, labels, and classifications shape inequality, and how we might measure racialization in ways that serve equity rather than entrench hierarchy.

Teaching

At UC Berkeley I teach undergraduate and graduate courses in Chicanx Latinx Studies and Ethnic Studies:

  • Race and Latinxs — undergraduate seminar
  • Latinx Data Lab: Bay Area Communities by the Numbers — undergraduate
  • Latino Social Science Thought — graduate
  • Social Structures: Contemporary Theories and Methods — graduate

Previously, I taught courses on race and ethnicity, Latinx Studies, migration, racism in education, and sociology at the University of Florida (2015–2023), the University of California, Merced (2012–2014), and Purdue University (2011–2012).

Advising & Mentorship

Mentorship is central to my work. I have chaired 19 graduate committees and served on 16 more, advising students whose research spans Latinx racialization, immigration, higher education, race and data, and related topics in sociology and ethnic studies.

Prospective graduate students interested in working with me should apply through the Department of Ethnic Studies Ph.D. program and are welcome to mention my name and research interests in your application.
